THE ANSWER

What the sources show

Across 2Q–4Q 2023, Tesla framed Optimus as an AI-driven humanoid where car AI learnings translate to robotics, while acknowledging key development barriers (notably lack of suppliers for appropriate humanoid actuators and the need to design its own actuators). In 3Q 2023 management emphasized the goal of useful capability and “periodic updates online,” while referencing rapid progress (from barely walking to doing yoga). In 1Q 2024 management shifted toward concrete near-term production expectations, stating Optimus would be in limited production in Tesla’s factory doing useful tasks before year-end, with possible external sales by end of next year (described as guesses). By 2Q 2024, Tesla added operational evidence that Optimus began performing tasks autonomously in a facility (handling batteries), and provided a more detailed timeline: Version 1 limited production early 2025, Version 2 high-volume production produced in Texas toward the end of 2025, with outside-customer provision in 2026. In 4Q 2024, Tesla further specified internal production plans and economics (including a target production cost under $20,000 at steady state above 1 million units/year) while also saying the design is rapidly evolving and not design-locked for 2025.

Watch points

  • Timeline uncertainty and changing specificity: 1Q 2024 external sales timing and factory timelines were explicitly described as “just guesses,” and earlier periods stated management was “not ready to discuss details” while still offering high-level trajectory comments.
  • Potential inconsistency between “not design-locked” evolution and defined production milestones (4Q 2024 says design is rapidly evolving and not design-locked for 2025, while also discussing pilot/production expectations and line targets).
  • Economic/scale claims evolve from qualitative “more valuable than everything else combined” to more quantitative targets (e.g., unit cost under $20,000 at steady state), but the excerpts do not show accompanying verified financial impact or audited metrics beyond internal planning statements.
  • Utility barrier: getting the robot to do something useful at scale (repeated emphasis).
  • Supply-chain/engineering bottleneck for humanoid actuators (no suitable off-the-shelf options).
  • Disclosure caution around program details (management not ready to discuss details; reliance on periodic updates online).
  • Safety/existential risk framing contingent on a future “super-sentient” capability (terminator-level risk mentioned).
  • Scaling risk implied by the repeated focus on “utility” and manufacturing ramp/production costs.
